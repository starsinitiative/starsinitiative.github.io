Who is Responsible for Space Trash Damages?

3 MIN READ · STARS TEAM · SEPTEMBER 2026

In 1978, a Soviet satellite carrying a nuclear reactor re-entered our planet’s atmosphere and disintegrated over northern Canada, tearing apart into hundreds of pieces. Canada needed to search for and clean up the fragments found on Canadian territory because the radioactive debris had scattered over a large area. Canada relied on the Liability Convention to request that the Soviet Union pay approximately six million Canadian dollars for the cleanup costs. The Liability Convention says that the launching state is absolutely liable for damage its objects cause on Earth, so Canada did not have to prove fault by the Soviet Union. In the end, the Soviet Union agreed to pay three million Canadian dollars in 1981 to settle the issue.

The Liability Convention says that the launching state is absolutely liable for damage its objects cause on Earth, so Canada did not have to prove fault by the Soviet Union.

As it can be understood from this example, global space organizations have many basic rules they agree to allow for proper liability of actions that cause harm to other spacecraft or locations on Earth. Firstly, countries are responsible for their national space activities, including those of their private companies; a country needs to authorize and supervise its private space operators. Secondly, as previously stated, a launching state is absolutely liable for damage its space objects cause on Earth or to aircraft. In the Liability Convention, a “launching state” is defined as the state that launches the object, arranges the launch, or from whose territory or facility it is launched. Lastly, another basic law is that if one space object damages another in space, the launching state is only responsible if it or someone who is responsible for it was at fault. However, an issue with this law is that the treaty never fully defines what “at fault” means, making some liability decisions difficult.

Even though these basic rules have been set in place, there are many confusions, especially with modern-day cases. One of the main issues is quite clear: many missions have multiple companies and countries involved. While the Liability Convention states that the launching state is responsible, a case could be quite difficult if, for example, one country arranges a launch, another provides the launch facility, and a private company from a third country designs the payload. The payload company’s country would not automatically be a launching state just because it designed the payload.

Moreover, there is no simple formula for what a responsible country needs to pay. The Convention states compensation should restore the affected party to the condition it would have been in if the damage had not happened. Even though the Soviet Union and Canada were able to settle their issue, no standard formula for every case was set.

The growing role of commercial spacecraft makes liability more challenging. In the United States, commercial launch and reentry operators need to be licensed by the FAA to launch or reenter objects. This licensing process can require operators to show financial responsibility, but it does not remove the government's international treaty responsibility.

Space law such as the Liability Convention provides a starting point for assigning responsibility, but proving fault, calculating damages, and authorizing debris removal remain difficult. Clearer, more modern rules could help protect spacecraft and make accountability fairer in the aerospace industry.
