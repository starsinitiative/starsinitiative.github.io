How Is Space Trash Tracked?

2 MIN READ · STARS TEAM · SEPTEMBER 2026

Space trash is a growing problem in the 21st century. Currently, scientists estimate that there are over 170 million pieces of space debris larger than 1 millimeter in Earth’s orbit. However, less than 1% of these pieces are fully trackable. This is because current tracking systems can only catalog objects larger than about 5–10 centimeters in low Earth orbit and 30–100 centimeters in geosynchronous orbit. Smaller debris cannot be tracked, and its existence and location are merely estimated from its effects.

However, less than 1% of these pieces are fully trackable.

The Space Surveillance Network (SSN), part of the U.S. Space Force, tracks space trash in Earth’s orbit. This network tracks identifiable objects through its specialized radar and optical sensors found in various sites throughout the world. These sensors also track how the objects move, including collisions, approaches, and reentries. SSN radar detects objects by bouncing radio waves off them, while the optical sensors use telescopes to observe reflected sunlight and estimate an object’s path over time. While other countries also have advanced space tracking systems, the SSN provides data to many countries and their satellite owners as well.

For example, the United States Space Command (USSPACECOM) releases publicly available space object tracking data through space-track.org, including active satellites, rocket parts, dysfunctional spacecraft, and space debris with sizes larger than the tracking limits. Unfortunately, the amount of space debris analyzed is significantly lower than what exists because the specialized radar and optical sensors are not able to detect smaller debris. Moreover, NASA’s Conjunction Assessment Risk Analysis (CARA) provides possible threats and collision-risk assessments for NASA spacecraft. CARA uses catalogued data provided by the SSN to help NASA missions assess close approaches and decide whether to take action.

Unfortunately, it is quite clear that smaller space debris are almost impossible to track. In recent years, engineers and scientists have been making estimates on the amount of smaller space debris in Earth’s orbit through the use of many radar installations. Some of the radar installations are able to detect smaller objects when they pass through their radar beam. However, these installations are unable to determine the space debris’ orbits. Still, scientists can estimate the number of pieces of small space debris in our planet’s orbit by compiling the amount of radar intersections over a short period of time.

Engineers have found debris impacts on spacecraft surfaces returned to Earth, including parts from the Hubble Space Telescope. Using this data from multiple sources has allowed us to estimate the amount of space debris in space. Engineers and scientists also use models to simulate collisions and breakups of objects in space.

Although tracking systems can identify many larger objects in orbit, millions of smaller fragments remain difficult to locate individually. Scientists rely on radar observations, satellite inspections, and computer models to estimate their numbers. Improving debris detection is important because better information can help protect satellites and prevent future collisions in space.
