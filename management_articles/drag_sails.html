Space Traffic Management

Drag Sails:

3 MIN READ · STARS TEAM · OCTOBER 2026

There are currently over 43,000 large objects in orbit around Earth. Scientists also estimate that there are around 1.22 million pieces of space debris larger than one centimeter and more than 130 million microscopic particles orbiting our planet. Unfortunately, this issue will continue to grow exponentially if we do not find solutions to mitigate this expanding problem.

Many aerospace engineers have worked together to find a possible solution: drag sails. A drag sail is a thin-film membrane that is held in shape by very light, deployable booms. The idea behind a drag sail uses the concept of increasing the surface area of a satellite. Once the satellite is regarded as “defunct,” engineers can deploy this drag sail from a small, compact compartment aboard the satellite.

As the sail expands, it comes into contact with more atmospheric molecules. This increases atmospheric drag, which greatly reduces the amount of time—often otherwise decades or even centuries—that it takes for a satellite to deorbit. Additionally, this solution is particularly useful when engineers do not want to waste extra fuel on deorbiting a satellite through propulsion or when the satellite simply needs an inexpensive way to be removed from Earth’s orbit.

However, this solution cannot resolve the issue of space debris on its own because there are several limitations. Drag sails are perfect for satellites in low Earth orbit, specifically between 600 and 800 kilometers in altitude. This is the region where spacecraft would otherwise take decades to deorbit, but where there are enough atmospheric particles for a drag sail to accelerate the spacecraft’s deorbit timeline. This would not be the case in other orbital locations. Another limitation is that drag sails might encounter issues when they begin to move and are no longer perpendicular to the direction of travel. The effectiveness of the drag sails noticeably decreases in these instances.

Drag sails demonstrate that preventing space debris does not always require complicated or fuel-intensive technology.

On the other hand, multiple organizations have already implemented drag sails into their satellites. For example, many groups from Purdue University and the University of Alabama have conducted extensive research on experimental drag sails over the past decade. NASA itself launched NanoSail-D2, a nanosatellite the size of a water bottle. It deployed a drag sail that was 10 meters wide, quickly decreasing the nanosatellite’s estimated deorbit time from 50 years to less than a year.

Drag sails demonstrate that preventing space debris does not always require complicated or fuel-intensive technology. By increasing a satellite’s surface area, these lightweight devices use atmospheric drag to pull inactive spacecraft out of low Earth orbit, where they safely burn up during reentry. Although drag sails cannot remove existing debris or operate effectively in every orbit, including them on future satellites could reduce collisions and prevent the creation of even more debris. As Earth’s orbital environment becomes increasingly crowded, designing satellites with a responsible end-of-life plan will be essential to keeping space accessible for future exploration. For this reason, incorporating drag sails into future spacecraft could become a simple but important step toward making satellite operations more sustainable in the long term
