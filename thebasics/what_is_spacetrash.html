What is Space Trash?

2 MIN READ · STARS TEAM · AUGUST 2026

Every day, thousands of satellites orbit silently above us, helping us navigate, communicate, and study our planet. Sharing that same space are millions of pieces of human-made debris—turning Earth's orbit into an increasingly crowded and dangerous environment. Many scientists describe space debris, also called space trash or orbital debris, as the “leftovers” of the impressive spacecraft we launch into our planet’s orbit. In fact, NASA even states that “LEO [Low Earth Orbit] is an orbital space junkyard.”

Space debris refers to any human-made object in Earth's orbit that no longer serves a useful purpose. These objects include small rocket components, flakes of paint, remnants of exploded spacecraft, tools lost by astronauts, defunct satellites, and much more. These pieces of trash are hazardous to operational spacecraft and satellites.

“LEO [Low Earth Orbit] is an orbital space junkyard.”

In just the past 60 years, there have been more than 40,500 pieces of space debris larger than 4 inches recorded by the European Space Agency (ESA). The ESA also estimates that about 1.1 million particles between 0.4 and 4 inches in size, along with hundreds of millions of smaller particles, are currently orbiting Earth. The U.S. military currently tracks more than 20,000 pieces of debris larger than 4 inches, with the total mass of these objects exceeding 93,000 tons. As more rockets and satellites are launched into orbit, experts estimate that the number of large tracked objects could eventually exceed 100,000.

Space debris exists in a variety of forms, ranging from millimeter-sized particles to objects several meters across. 24% of the tracked space debris in our planet’s orbit are satellites. 11% of the objects are mission-related objects such as discarded rocket stages, launch adapters, and lens covers. Many of the smallest particles of space debris are created during collisions, explosions, or fragmentation events involving satellites and rocket stages. These particles can be paint flecks, coolant drops, bolts, screws, or wiring.

Space debris has devastating power. These particles are spinning in orbit between 15,000 and 20,000 miles per hour. Even the smallest object, smaller than a grain of rice, carries enough energy as a truck traveling at 70 miles per hour. This is more than enough energy to destroy a satellite or spacecraft.

Many space organizations across the globe have recognized the importance of tracking space debris. One example is the Space Surveillance Network (SSN) operated by the U.S. Air Force. The SSN uses radar and optical sensors to observe objects larger than a softball in Low Earth Orbit and objects larger than a basketball in geosynchronous orbit. Additionally, spacecraft that return from orbit often show visible impact marks caused by tiny pieces of debris, and organizations such as NASA and the ESA collaborate to collect data using this visual information.

Although most people never see it, space debris has become a permanent part of Earth's orbital environment. Understanding what it is, where it comes from, and how it is tracked is the first step toward ensuring that future space missions can continue safely.

- STARS Team
